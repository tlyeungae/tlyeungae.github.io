Iam a PhD candidate in the Department of Economics at USI. I received my bachelor’s degree in financial engineering from 武漢大學 (WHU) in 2014 and a research master’s in finance from 中國社會科學院研究生院 (GSCASS) in 2019, where I studied at the Institute of Finance and Banking. That training sparked my lasting interest in China’s financial development and economic policy. I then earned a master’s degree in economics from 香港科大商學院 (HKUST Business School) in 2020 and another master’s degree in economics and finance from Barcelona School of Economics (UPF) in 2021 with a full tuition waiver. I was a visiting scholar in the Finance Department at the Wharton School (UPenn), from January to June 2025, hosted by Prof. Sylvain Catherine.

I am a financial economist working in household finance. I study how households turn earnings into wealth and how they use that wealth when circumstances change, with a focus on earnings risk, experience and memory, and the institutions governing saving and housing.

Curriculum Vitæ [updated September 2026]

Research

Resources alone do not determine financial choices. Earnings prospects, the content of past experience, and the rules governing what households can retain or transfer also matter. I combine longitudinal surveys in the United States and Japan with investor and housing transaction records in China, using empirical evidence and economic models to study market participation, saving, and asset sales. My interest in China’s financial development and policy connects this agenda to housing markets and school rights.

01

Earnings risk and wealth accumulation

How do earnings opportunities and setbacks translate into financial wealth? My job market paper separates upside earnings opportunities from downside risk in market entry. Related projects examine personal financial buffers after weak labor-market starts, retirement cash-outs around job changes, and earnings adjustment under social insurance mandates.

02

Experience and financial choice

How do gains, losses, and decision context affect financial choices? I study investors’ responses to past outcomes through models of memory retrieval, and compare choices in self-directed and delegated accounts. Related work asks what repeated preference questions reveal about future wealth beyond information already available in household balance sheets.

03

Housing liquidity and transferable rights

When can households turn housing wealth into usable resources? Using Beijing listing histories and transactions, I examine how valuation difficulty, buyer disagreement, and seller adjustment relate to prices and selling time. A complementary project studies school rights that owners retain but cannot transfer to buyers, asking why protecting continued use need not preserve the gains from exchange.

→ household wealth, housing markets, and Chinese policy

Beyond Variance: Asymmetric Labor Income Risk and Portfolio Entry

At a given earnings variance, upside opportunities predict greater stock-market entry and entry followed by sustained ownership; a life-cycle model links the opposing tail patterns to saving and costly market access, while reproducing only part of their magnitude.
Download PDF | SSRN

Abstract: The division of earnings risk between favorable and adverse outcomes predicts stock-market entry beyond total variance. I measure earnings distributions in pre-portfolio Current Population Survey histories and relate their upper and lower partial variances to subsequent portfolio transitions in the Survey of Income and Program Participation. At a given total variance, a one-standard-deviation increase in upside composition predicts 1.6 percentage points more entry into stock and mutual-fund holdings, nearly one fifth of the sample entry rate. The upper tail predicts more entry and the lower tail less. In the longer-follow-up cohort, upside composition also predicts entry followed by sustained ownership at the next two annual observations. A participation-cost model illustrates how favorable realizations create access at unchanged mean and variance. A life-cycle model with measured retirement resources, calibrated to financial transitions, generates the opposing tail signs without targeting an earnings-risk coefficient, but reproduces only part of the entry gradient. The findings establish a distributional dimension of market access that earnings variance alone misses and provide participation moments for household-finance models.

Presented at: Fourth Georgia Tech – Atlanta Fed Household Finance Conference (Georgia Tech, 2026), AFA 2026 (Philadelphia, 2026), 19th International Behavioural Finance Conference (Booth School of Business, 2025), 14th International Moscow Finance Conference (HSE Moscow, 2025), SFI PhD Student Workshop (University of Zurich, 2025), 10th Luxembourg Workshop on Household Finance and Consumption (Central Bank of Luxembourg, 2025), German Finance Association (DGF) Annual Meeting 2025 Doctoral Workshop (University of Hagen, 2025), Gerzensee Alumni Conference (Study Center Gerzensee, 2024, 2025), Macro Finance Research Program (MFR) 2024 Summer Session for Young Scholars (University of Chicago, 2024), Frankfurt Summer School 2024 (The Deutsche Bundesbank, 2024), 2nd Workshop on Applied Macroeconomics and Monetary Policy (University of St. Gallen, 2024), RES PhD Conference 2024 (University of Portsmouth, 2024), USI IdEP Brown Bag (USI, 2023, 2024, 2025)

Navigating Through Fear and Greed: The Experience-Driven Disposition Effect

Past realized losses are associated with a stronger disposition effect and gains with a weaker one; a memory-based recall model reproduces the asymmetry without changing preferences.
Download PDF | SSRN | Slides
with Rong Liu (TJU), Jessica Wachter (Wharton), Michael Kahana (UPenn) and Yongjie Zhang (TJU)

Abstract: Using transaction-level data on 189,530 Chinese retail investors over the 2013–2016 boom and bust, we measure experience as counts of large realized gains and losses and examine how these counts relate to the disposition effect, the tendency to sell winners and hold losers. A two-standard-deviation increase in accumulated large losses raises the propensity to sell winners rather than losers by about 23% of the baseline effect, while an equivalent increase in gains lowers it by about 21%. Under composition-matched simulations, experience-independent selling rules do not reproduce this asymmetry. To explain both the baseline effect and this asymmetry, we build a memory-based recall model in which similarity-weighted retrieval of past outcomes guides selling. A single retrieval channel reproduces all three patterns without shifting preferences.

Presented at: Finance Brownbag (University of Bristol, 2026), Workshop on Cognitive Economics (University of Zurich, 2026), SFS Cavalcade North America (University of Virginia, 2026), Memory, Beliefs, and Choice (MBC) (University of Pennsylvania, 2025), USI IdEP Brown Bag (USI, 2024)

When Price Stops Clearing: Valuation Disagreement and Housing Illiquidity

Harder-to-value homes sell more slowly and at lower prices, with fewer linked sales during follow-up; a quantitative model attributes most fitted delay to incomplete seller adjustment to lower valuations.

Abstract: Why do some homes remain difficult to sell even at lower prices? I study valuation difficulty and housing liquidity using 354,502 completed Beijing resales and a separate cohort of 16,108 active listings. Configuration rarity and prior comparable-sale scarcity provide measures that do not use the home's own price. A full-unit increase in the within-community–month rarity rank corresponds to approximately 16–27 percent longer selling time and 1.5–3.4 percent lower prices, depending on attribute controls. In the active cohort, the corresponding gap in the probability of a linked same-spell sale during follow-up is 3.5–8.0 percentage points. Negotiated price gaps have larger absolute deviations from local means. Among listings without recorded revisions, the asking-price–duration slope falls from 2.44 to 0.48 across submarket-dispersion quintiles. A search model interprets these patterns through average willingness to pay, buyer disagreement, and seller reservation values. Its quantitative decomposition retains a positive price contribution from optimistic buyer selection, offset by a larger valuation discount. Most of the fitted delay arises when sellers adjust incompletely to that discount. The results connect valuation difficulty to the price, speed, and completion of housing transactions.

Presented at: Rising Scholar Conference in Finance (University of Zurich, 2026), UEA Summer School (LSE, 2026), USI IdEP Brown Bag (USI, 2024)

Scarred Balance Sheets: Labor-Market Entry and Women's Own Financial Buffers in Japan

Data: Panel Data Research Center (PDRC) at Keio University, Project ID: 10446
Weaker early labor markets are associated with smaller personal financial buffers among Japanese women, mainly deposits; common-age comparisons do not establish faster accumulation at equal income.

Abstract: Do early labor markets leave a financial legacy beyond the earnings measured later in a career? I study women's own deposits and securities over 29 waves of the Japanese Panel Survey of Consumers, using a consistent measure before and after marriage. Comparing childhood prefectures within predicted-entry cohorts, a one-standard-deviation weaker local market is associated with about 150,000 yen less in later personal financial assets, ten percent of the mean. Deposits account for almost all of the difference. A birth-cohort population defined independently of schooling preserves a positive relationship with mean conditions at ages 18, 20 and 22, equivalent to about 105,000 yen; the age-18 estimate is less precise. Favorable conditions over these ages also accompany more regular employment. Comparisons of assets and resources over common five-year age windows do not establish faster accumulation at equal income. The results connect early labor-market conditions to the financial assets women report as their own, measured consistently through marriage and career interruptions.

Data: Japanese Panel Survey of Consumers (JPSC), Panel Data Research Center at Keio University

Twice-Told Preferences? Repeated Elicitation and Household Wealth

Repeated answers add predictive information when baseline wealth is unavailable, but almost none when it is observed; the wealth gradient also depends on how response categories are represented.

Abstract: What does asking again add to a household-finance survey? I link two to six early intertemporal-choice responses from 3,099 Japanese households to financial wealth six to eight years after a baseline balance sheet. Compared with a flexible first answer, repeated category shares reduce held-out prediction error by an additional 1.57 percentage points of control-model loss when baseline wealth is unavailable, but only 0.008 points when it is included. The conditional gain varies across evaluation samples. A separate measurement problem remains: the displayed-rate wealth gradient depends heavily on the upper response category, and flexible category profiles reject its linear restriction. Repeated answers contain information about future reported financial position, but their predictive value depends on the information already observed and how the answers are represented.

Data: Panel Data Research Center (PDRC) at Keio University, Project ID: 10446

WORKING PAPER

When Jobs End: Layoffs, Quits, and Retirement Cash-Outs

Retirement cash-outs accompany voluntary job changes even without an employment interruption, and the voluntary-separation gap is larger among workers with limited prior financial assets.

Abstract: Retirement distributions can accompany job changes without an interruption of employment. Using 39,481 SIPP person-years of prior workers with reported 401(k) or thrift ownership, I find that voluntary separators holding a job throughout the year have a 1.67-percentage-point higher non-rollover rate than continuers (95-percent interval [0.68,2.67]). Their median year-on-year change in nominal all-job earnings was 11 percent. In a separate resource comparison, the voluntary-separation gap is 3.76 points below one month of prior earnings in financial assets and 0.42 points above that threshold. The 3.34-point interaction survives multiple-testing adjustment and appears in both analysis windows. These observational findings place retirement cash-outs at the intersection of job mobility and limited financial resources; they do not identify the effects of separation or the use of withdrawn funds.

Social Insurance Mandates and Earnings Adjustment: Evidence from Japan

with Yi Yao (USI)
Data: Panel Data Research Center (PDRC) at Keio University, Project ID: 9763
Adjustment to Japan’s employer insurance mandate differs with prior household coverage: dependent spouses show a larger announcement-to-enforcement decline in earnings-band membership than direct contributors.

Abstract: An employer insurance mandate can introduce a new household payment for a dependent spouse while replacing contributions already paid by another worker. We study this distinction in Japan's 2016 expansion of employee social insurance using longitudinal data on married part-time women. Employer-size exposure and pension status are fixed before the 2012 announcement. From announcement to enforcement, the large-versus-small-firm gap in membership in the ¥1.06–1.29 million earnings band falls 21.1 percentage points more for dependent spouses than for workers already contributing directly. Separately reported earnings from all jobs yield a 15.4-point estimate. Documented nonenrolled employment at 20 or more weekly hours also declines differentially. The earnings pattern reverses an announcement-period increase; net changes from the earlier baseline are less precisely estimated. Together, the findings document how the timing and distribution of adjustment under an employer mandate differ with workers' pre-existing household insurance arrangements.

WORKING PAPER

Grandfathering at Sale: Nonportable School Rights and Housing Exchange

After a sale resets school rights, relative sales growth is 32–45% lower and transaction prices 4–5% lower in formerly key-school neighborhoods; lower prices need not restore trade.

Abstract: Can lower prices sustain housing trade when buyers cannot acquire the school rights enjoyed by existing owners? I study a Beijing reform that preserved the previous admission rules for eligible homeowners, while new buyers faced assignment across several schools. Linking enrollment notices to housing transactions, I compare winter-to-spring changes around January 2019 with the same seasons in earlier years. Sales remain roughly flat in formerly key-school neighborhoods but rise in ordinary-school neighborhoods. After adjusting for earlier seasonal patterns, the gap in relative sales growth is 32–45 percent; transaction prices are also 4–5 percent lower relative to ordinary-school neighborhoods. A matching model explains why price reductions need not restore trade when a sale changes the rights attached to a home. The model also shows that portable school rights can facilitate exchange while reducing other families' access when school places are fixed.

Industrial Composition and Marriage Timing in China’s Third Front

with Yi Yao (USI)
Heavy-industry exposure is associated with greater young singlehood inside Third Front provinces, but cohort comparisons do not establish a causal effect of the construction campaign.

Abstract: Industrial employment can facilitate marriage through stable earnings while delaying household formation when jobs, housing, and residence are allocated together. We study this tension in China's Third Front using the 1982, 1990, and 2000 population censuses. In 1982, a 13.7-percentage-point increase in the registered heavy-industry share is associated with a 4.23-point larger gap in never-married rates between ages 20–24 and 30–34 inside designated provinces than elsewhere. Predetermined siting controls reduce the differential to 2.24 points. Retrospective first-marriage histories observed in 2000 yield a similar age profile, and education shares account for 15.5 percent of a comparable young–old gap. However, comparisons of pre-campaign and build-out cohorts do not yield a robust additional marriage shift inside Third Front provinces. The evidence documents a recurring association between industrial composition and young singlehood, rather than a causal effect of the construction campaign. It distinguishes a demographic pattern among residents of industrial places from an effect assigned to people exposed to a particular industrial policy.

Presented at: GLO-Guangzhou-2026 (IESR, 2026), USI IdEP Brown Bag (USI, 2026)

WORK IN PROGRESSw/1

Whose Loss Is It?

with Chaojie (Jay) Liu (Bristol)
Preliminary evidence compares the same investor across self-directed and robo-advised accounts, asking whether who chose the holdings changes how a gain or loss is acted upon.

Preliminary abstract: Does the same investor treat a loss differently when it is hers rather than an algorithm’s? We study 100,000 investors on Alipay’s Ant Fortune platform (September 2020 to September 2024) who simultaneously hold a self-directed mutual-fund portfolio and a robo-advisory account (BangNiTou). Comparing the same investor across two accounts that differ only in who chose the holdings, we find that the response to losses does not weaken under delegation—it reverses sign. On her own account she holds and adds to losers (averaging down); on the delegated account she sells losers and chases winners. A single loss-aversion parameter cannot produce this sign flip. It follows, we argue, from delegation changing what a return means: on her own account a loss is processed through realization utility, while on the delegated account it is both a signal about the algorithm and a source of dissonance she resolves by exiting. We estimate the model structurally and quantify the return investors forgo through self-directed loss aversion. Full empirical results remain in progress.

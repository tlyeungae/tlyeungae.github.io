I study how lived experience becomes memory—and how that memory changes the financial decisions households make today. I also study how policy and institutions, particularly in China, shape household and market outcomes.

Curriculum Vitæ [updated August 2026]

Research

I am a financial economist working at the intersection of household finance, behavioral finance, and the Chinese economy. My research asks how experience and memory shape current decisions, and how financial development, policy, and institutions alter the opportunities and constraints households and markets face.

01

Experience becomes memory

Past gains and losses, recessions, and market crashes do not enter every decision mechanically. I study which experiences people retrieve, how they connect them, and how long those memories remain economically active.

02

Memory shapes beliefs

Experience matters through internal states we rarely observe directly. I examine subjective earnings risk and repeated preference elicitations, asking what these measures capture and what changes when measurement is noisy.

03

Households decide within institutions

I trace these forces into portfolios, trading, saving, retirement, and housing, and study how policy changes the choices available. My work on China uses policy reforms and historical industrialization to understand financial development, market exchange, and household formation.

→ portfolios, trading, saving, retirement, housing, and policy

// evidence — administrative records, transaction histories, policy variation, and long household panels from the U.S., China, and Japan

WORKING PAPER

Grandfathering at Sale: Nonportable School Rights and Housing Exchange

When a sale resets a grandfathered school right, completed housing flow falls sharply relative to ordinary communities, while public active-stock and asking-price comparisons move less.
Download PDF | SSRN

Abstract: When a public entitlement is grandfathered only while an asset is held, selling forfeits what holding preserves. I study Beijing’s January 2019 title-vintage rule, under which transfers moved buyers from a single-school mapping to multi-school assignment. In 54 exactly linked communities, formerly key and ordinary communities both begin near three platform completions per 1,000 Lianjia-recorded housing units. Over the next sixteen weeks, ordinary communities rise to about six while formerly key communities remain near three. The raw exposed-relative PPML ratio is 0.57–0.61; seasonal benchmarks imply 0.42–0.67, with the largest gap against an unusually strong 2018. The negative sign survives frozen samples, homogeneous completion clocks, count estimators, and every school and community deletion. Public active-stock and asking-price comparisons move less, conditional on unverified crawler comparability, but do not identify a listing hazard. A search-and-bargaining model shows how a sale-triggered entitlement reset can remove marginal matches without requiring a commensurate selected-price response.

Beyond Variance: Asymmetric Labor Income Risk and Portfolio Entry

Assigned lower and upper earnings tails have opposite conditional associations with later positive reported holdings; total span is imprecise, and excluding self-employed destination records sharply attenuates the lower-tail estimate.
Download PDF | SSRN

Abstract: Variance compresses favorable and adverse earnings realizations into one scale measure. I use public CPS earnings histories and strictly later SIPP portfolios to separate lower-tail distance, upper-tail distance, total span, and orientation. Among 27,752 annual transitions beginning with no reported stock-and-mutual-fund holdings, a one-standard-deviation wider lower tail predicts a 2.71-percentage-point lower probability of positive holdings one year later and a wider upper tail a 1.92-point higher probability; the pair remains jointly distinguishable from zero under a 200-draw CPS first-stage sensitivity. With 99,999 cell-level wild-cluster draws, the lower and upper coefficients have two-component adjusted q-values of 0.019 and 0.087, and the signed composition contrast has p = 0.033. An exact rotation leaves total span imprecise and composition negative. Recomputing the CPS tails after excluding destination records classified as self-employed sharply attenuates the lower estimate. Across alternative zero-endpoint constructions, composition retains its direction and conditional precision; the standalone upper estimate remains positive but is imprecise in one construction. Historical group distributions are assigned rather than elicited, so the estimates are descriptive.

Navigating Through Fear and Greed: The Experience-Driven Disposition Effect

Past losses make investors quicker to sell winners, past gains slower. The channel is memory retrieval, not a change in preferences.
Download PDF | SSRN | Slides
with Rong Liu (TJU), Jessica Wachter (Wharton), Michael Kahana (UPenn) and Yongjie Zhang (TJU)

Abstract: Using transaction-level data on 189,530 Chinese retail investors over the 2013–2016 boom and bust, we measure experience as counts of large realized gains and losses and find that it reshapes the disposition effect, the tendency to sell winners and hold losers, in an asymmetric way. A two-standard-deviation increase in accumulated large losses raises the propensity to sell winners rather than losers by about 23% of the baseline effect, while an equivalent increase in gains lowers it by about 21%. Composition-matched simulations show the asymmetry is not a mechanical artifact of building experience from past trades. To explain both the baseline effect and this asymmetry, we build a memory-based recall model in which similarity-weighted retrieval of past outcomes guides selling. A single retrieval channel reproduces all three patterns without shifting preferences.

Presented at: Finance Brownbag (University of Bristol, 2026), Workshop on Cognitive Economics (University of Zurich, 2026), SFS Cavalcade North America (University of Virginia, 2026), Memory, Beliefs, and Choice (MBC) (University of Pennsylvania, 2025), USI IdEP Brown Bag (USI, 2024)

Twice-Told Preferences? Repeated Elicitation, Response Coding, and Future Household Wealth

Repeated responses make persistence and coding assumptions visible, but add virtually no out-of-sample prediction beyond the first category; the apparent wealth gradient is driven by the questionnaire’s 40% endpoint.
Download PDF | SSRN

Abstract: Repeated elicitation is commonly used to improve the measurement of economic preferences. I test that premise using 56,050 intertemporal-choice responses from 6,808 Japanese households. Responses observed through 2015 predict financial wealth in 2022–2024 conditional on 2016 wealth, allowing a common-sample comparison of one response, averages, response distributions, and split-block IV. Averaging produces a negative displayed-rate gradient, and split-block IV makes it steeper. Yet the gradient is driven by the questionnaire’s 40% endpoint: capping that category makes the slope imprecise, and flexible category models reject displayed-rate linearity. Moreover, post-first responses add virtually no out-of-sample prediction beyond the first category. Repetition therefore makes persistence and coding assumptions visible, but does not produce a demonstrably better predictor of future wealth. The estimates are predictive rather than causal. The result is a boundary condition for treating repeated survey responses as increasingly precise measures of latent preferences.

Data: Panel Data Research Center (PDRC) at Keio University, Project ID: 9763

When Jobs End: Layoffs, Quits, and Retirement Cash-Outs

Retirement cash-outs rise around both quits and involuntary separations relative to continued work, while the involuntary–voluntary difference is imprecise; the comparison group is central to interpreting a layoff coefficient.
Download PDF | SSRN

Abstract: An involuntary job loss can reduce household liquidity, while any job ending can alter how employer-plan balances are handled. I use two regimes of the Survey of Income and Program Participation and direct reports of pension or retirement lump sums and rollover plans to study annual non-rollover distributions. A conventional permanent-layoff–no-layoff comparison yields a 1.74-percentage-point association, relative to a 0.79-percent mean. Holding the sample and specification fixed produces a 1.55-point permanent-layoff contrast and a 0.40-point involuntary–voluntary separation contrast. Among prior workers reporting a 401(k) or thrift account, both voluntary and involuntary separators have higher non-rollover incidence than continuers, while their difference is imprecise. Outcome accounting shows that receipt rises for both groups and that most of each receipt difference is accounted for by non-rollover. Non-rollover is therefore elevated around separation broadly, and the treatment-and-comparison definition is central to interpreting a layoff coefficient.

Industrial Composition and Marriage Timing in China’s Third Front

with Yi Yao (USI)
Industrial composition is associated with a persistent marriage-age gradient across three censuses and retrospective cohort histories, but current residence and campaign comparisons do not identify a causal Third Front effect.
Download PDF

Abstract: Did industrialization under central planning leave a distinctive marriage-age profile? We study China’s Third Front with the 1982, 1990, and 2000 population censuses. In 1982, a one-standard-deviation increase in the heavy-industry share is associated with a 4.23-percentage-point larger gap in never marriage between ages 20–24 and 30–34 inside Third Front provinces than elsewhere; predetermined siting controls reduce the differential to 2.24 points. Retrospective first-marriage histories observed in 2000 yield similar pseudo-1982 point estimates under birth-year and completed-age indexing. The point-estimate alignment survives on identical respondents, although uncertainty permits meaningful differences and current residence cannot recover the 1982 population. Campaign-aligned comparisons show an early marriage deficit and subsequent catch-up within industrial Third Front areas, but no robust additional shift relative to other areas. Education shares account for 15.5 percent of a comparable young–old gap. The paper establishes a persistent resident-population marriage-age gradient and a transparent bridge from census stocks to cohort histories, while showing why neither identifies the campaign’s causal effect.

A Cold Start: Recession Entry and the Purposes of Household Saving

Moving from late-bubble to trough entry conditions predicts about 4,750 yen more monthly saving, roughly 87% designated for family-common, child, or other purposes, alongside fewer durable purchases and more discouraged borrowing.
Download PDF | SSRN

Abstract: How do labor-market conditions at school leaving shape what households later save for? I link 4,120 Japanese women observed in 29 waves of the JPSC to the national job-openings ratio at predicted graduation. Moving from late-bubble to trough entry conditions predicts 4,750 yen more monthly saving among married women with complete saving reports, about 7 percent of the sample mean. Roughly 87 percent of this response is designated for family-common, child, or other purposes rather than either spouse. Adverse entry also predicts fewer durable purchases and more discouraged borrowing, consistent with a defensive financial posture. Among positive savers, spouse-designated shares fall, but the wife–husband split does not move precisely; the share pattern is therefore an amount-decomposition result, not evidence of a transfer between spouses. The allocation contrast is robust to cohort clustering and cohort-score HAC inference and is extreme among circular treatment rotations, although studentized rotations and within-year designs are less decisive. The results extend labor-market scarring from household balance sheets to the purposes attached to saving.

Data: Japanese Panel Survey of Consumers (JPSC), Panel Data Research Center at Keio University

WORK IN PROGRESSw/1

Whose Loss Is It?

with Chaojie (Jay) Liu (Bristol)
Preliminary evidence compares the same investor across self-directed and robo-advised accounts, asking whether who chose the holdings changes how a gain or loss is acted upon.

Preliminary abstract: Does the same investor treat a loss differently when it is hers rather than an algorithm’s? We study 100,000 investors on Alipay’s Ant Fortune platform (September 2020 to September 2024) who simultaneously hold a self-directed mutual-fund portfolio and a robo-advisory account (BangNiTou). Comparing the same investor across two accounts that differ only in who chose the holdings, we find that the response to losses does not weaken under delegation—it reverses sign. On her own account she holds and adds to losers (averaging down); on the delegated account she sells losers and chases winners. A single loss-aversion parameter cannot produce this sign flip. It follows, we argue, from delegation changing what a return means: on her own account a loss is processed through realization utility, while on the delegated account it is both a signal about the algorithm and a source of dissonance she resolves by exiting. We estimate the model structurally and quantify the return investors forgo through self-directed loss aversion. Full empirical results remain in progress.

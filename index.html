I received my bachelor’s degree in financial engineering from 武漢大學 (WHU) in 2014 and a research master’s in finance from 中國社會科學院研究生院 (GSCASS) in 2019, where I studied at the Institute of Finance and Banking. I study how lived experience becomes memory—and how that memory changes the financial decisions households make today. I also study how policy and institutions, particularly in China, shape household and market outcomes.

Curriculum Vitæ [updated September 2026]

Research

I am a financial economist working at the intersection of household finance, behavioral finance, and the Chinese economy. My research asks how experience and memory shape current decisions, and how financial development, policy, and institutions alter the opportunities and constraints households and markets face.

01

Experience becomes memory

Past gains and losses, recessions, and market crashes do not enter every decision mechanically. I study which experiences people retrieve, how they connect them, and how long those memories remain economically active.

02

Memory shapes beliefs

Experience matters through internal states we rarely observe directly. I examine subjective earnings risk and repeated preference elicitations, asking what these measures capture and what changes when measurement is noisy.

03

Households decide within institutions

I trace these forces into portfolios, trading, saving, retirement, and housing, and study how policy changes the choices available. My work on China uses policy reforms and historical industrialization to understand financial development, market exchange, and household formation.

→ portfolios, trading, saving, retirement, housing, and policy

// evidence — administrative records, transaction histories, policy variation, and long household panels from the U.S., China, and Japan

Beyond Variance: Asymmetric Labor Income Risk and Portfolio Entry

At a given earnings variance, a more upside-oriented historical earnings distribution predicts greater subsequent portfolio entry; earnings-progression controls reduce the association, and inference depends on estimated-risk uncertainty.
Download PDF | SSRN

Abstract: At a given earnings variance, a more upside-oriented historical earnings distribution is associated with greater subsequent entry into stocks or mutual funds. I document this pattern by assigning earnings distributions from the Current Population Survey to later portfolios in the Survey of Income and Program Participation. Using validated earnings links and 27,419 portfolio transitions, a standard deviation of composition remaining after other regressors corresponds to 0.70 percentage point more entry for total earnings, or 0.66 when wage and total earnings destinations are positive. Lower and upper quantile tails also have opposite-signed entry associations. Adjusting for observable earnings progression reduces the estimated magnitudes, and inference is sensitive to how estimated-risk uncertainty is incorporated. The paper provides a portfolio-entry benchmark for household-finance models that distinguish earnings opportunities from adverse risk.

Presented at: Fourth Georgia Tech – Atlanta Fed Household Finance Conference (Georgia Tech, 2026), AFA 2026 (Philadelphia, 2026), 19th International Behavioural Finance Conference (Booth School of Business, 2025), 14th International Moscow Finance Conference (HSE Moscow, 2025), SFI PhD Student Workshop (University of Zurich, 2025), 10th Luxembourg Workshop on Household Finance and Consumption (Central Bank of Luxembourg, 2025), German Finance Association (DGF) Annual Meeting 2025 Doctoral Workshop (University of Hagen, 2025), Gerzensee Alumni Conference (Study Center Gerzensee, 2024, 2025), Macro Finance Research Program (MFR) 2024 Summer Session for Young Scholars (University of Chicago, 2024), Frankfurt Summer School 2024 (The Deutsche Bundesbank, 2024), 2nd Workshop on Applied Macroeconomics and Monetary Policy (University of St. Gallen, 2024), RES PhD Conference 2024 (University of Portsmouth, 2024), USI IdEP Brown Bag (USI, 2023, 2024, 2025)

Navigating Through Fear and Greed: The Experience-Driven Disposition Effect

Past losses make investors quicker to sell winners, past gains slower. The channel is memory retrieval, not a change in preferences.
Download PDF | SSRN | Slides
with Rong Liu (TJU), Jessica Wachter (Wharton), Michael Kahana (UPenn) and Yongjie Zhang (TJU)

Abstract: Using transaction-level data on 189,530 Chinese retail investors over the 2013–2016 boom and bust, we measure experience as counts of large realized gains and losses and find that it reshapes the disposition effect, the tendency to sell winners and hold losers, in an asymmetric way. A two-standard-deviation increase in accumulated large losses raises the propensity to sell winners rather than losers by about 23% of the baseline effect, while an equivalent increase in gains lowers it by about 21%. Composition-matched simulations show the asymmetry is not a mechanical artifact of building experience from past trades. To explain both the baseline effect and this asymmetry, we build a memory-based recall model in which similarity-weighted retrieval of past outcomes guides selling. A single retrieval channel reproduces all three patterns without shifting preferences.

Presented at: Finance Brownbag (University of Bristol, 2026), Workshop on Cognitive Economics (University of Zurich, 2026), SFS Cavalcade North America (University of Virginia, 2026), Memory, Beliefs, and Choice (MBC) (University of Pennsylvania, 2025), USI IdEP Brown Bag (USI, 2024)

A Cold Start: Recession Entry and the Purposes of Household Saving

Data: Panel Data Research Center (PDRC) at Keio University, Project ID: 10446
Weaker entry conditions predict 4,750 yen more September saving, about 4,110 yen outside spouse-specific categories; regional and education-specific comparisons do not independently corroborate the national-cohort pattern.

Abstract: Weaker labor-market conditions at entry are associated with more later saving designated for family-common, child, and other uses. I document this pattern using 29 waves of the Japanese Panel Survey of Consumers and 39 graduation cohorts. Among married respondents with complete reports, an entry-market decline comparable to the difference between late-bubble and trough cohorts predicts 4,750 yen more September household saving, about 7 percent of the sample mean. Approximately 4,110 yen of this difference falls outside the two spouse-specific categories. Share point estimates fall for both spouses after adverse entry, while their relative split is imprecisely estimated. Less frequent household-goods spending and more discouraged borrowing accompany the saving pattern. The comparison uses national entry conditions assigned by birth year and school-leaving age; regional and education-specific designs do not independently corroborate it. The findings show how the allocation of a saving flow can distinguish household adjustment that aggregate balances and individual shares alone leave unresolved.

Data: Japanese Panel Survey of Consumers (JPSC), Panel Data Research Center at Keio University

Twice-Told Preferences? Repeated Elicitation, Response Coding, and Future Household Wealth

Repeated answers add predictive information when baseline wealth is unavailable, but almost none when it is observed; the wealth gradient also depends on how response categories are represented.

Abstract: What does asking again add to a household-finance survey? I link two to six early intertemporal-choice responses from 3,099 Japanese households to financial wealth six to eight years after a baseline balance sheet. Compared with a flexible first answer, repeated category shares reduce held-out prediction error by an additional 1.57 percentage points of control-model loss when baseline wealth is unavailable, but only 0.008 points when it is included. The conditional gain varies across evaluation samples. A separate measurement problem remains: the displayed-rate wealth gradient depends heavily on the upper response category, and flexible category profiles reject its linear restriction. Repeated answers contain information about future reported financial position, but their predictive value depends on the information already observed and how the answers are represented.

Data: Panel Data Research Center (PDRC) at Keio University, Project ID: 10446

Social Insurance Mandates and Earnings Reallocation: Evidence from Japan

with Yi Yao (USI)
Data: Panel Data Research Center (PDRC) at Keio University, Project ID: 9763
Earnings adjustments around Japan’s insurance expansion differ by households’ prior contribution arrangements; coverage among current workers does not describe coverage in the initially exposed cohort.

Abstract: The household cost of an employer insurance mandate depends on how workers already finance coverage. We study Japan's 2016 expansion of employee social insurance using a cohort of married women initially employed part-time, with firm size and pension category fixed before the 2012 announcement. Between announcement and enforcement, the large-versus-small-firm gap in the probability of earning ¥1.06–1.29 million annually falls by 14.1 percentage points among dependent spouses and rises by an imprecise 7.0 points among workers already contributing directly. The difference is −21.1 points (standard error 8.9). This contrast reverses an announcement-period increase; the change from pre-announcement levels is imprecise. Differential earnings availability and sensitivity to the comparison group limit causal interpretation. Complementary evidence distinguishes enrollment among current part-time workers from enrollment in the original cohort. The findings document earnings adjustment across workers with different household financing arrangements and show why interpreting coverage expansion requires following the initially exposed population.

Industrial Composition and Marriage Timing in China’s Third Front

with Yi Yao (USI)
Heavy-industry exposure is associated with greater young singlehood inside Third Front provinces, but cohort comparisons do not establish a causal effect of the construction campaign.

Abstract: Industrial employment can facilitate marriage through stable earnings while delaying household formation when jobs, housing, and residence are allocated together. We study this tension in China's Third Front using the 1982, 1990, and 2000 population censuses. In 1982, a 13.7-percentage-point increase in the registered heavy-industry share is associated with a 4.23-point larger gap in never-married rates between ages 20–24 and 30–34 inside designated provinces than elsewhere. Predetermined siting controls reduce the differential to 2.24 points. Retrospective first-marriage histories observed in 2000 yield a similar age profile, and education shares account for 15.5 percent of a comparable young–old gap. However, comparisons of pre-campaign and build-out cohorts do not yield a robust additional marriage shift inside Third Front provinces. The evidence documents a recurring association between industrial composition and young singlehood, rather than a causal effect of the construction campaign. It distinguishes a demographic pattern among residents of industrial places from an effect assigned to people exposed to a particular industrial policy.

Presented at: GLO-Guangzhou-2026 (IESR, 2026), USI IdEP Brown Bag (USI, 2026)

Whose Loss Is It?

with Chaojie (Jay) Liu (Bristol)
Preliminary evidence compares the same investor across self-directed and robo-advised accounts, asking whether who chose the holdings changes how a gain or loss is acted upon.

Preliminary abstract: Does the same investor treat a loss differently when it is hers rather than an algorithm’s? We study 100,000 investors on Alipay’s Ant Fortune platform (September 2020 to September 2024) who simultaneously hold a self-directed mutual-fund portfolio and a robo-advisory account (BangNiTou). Comparing the same investor across two accounts that differ only in who chose the holdings, we find that the response to losses does not weaken under delegation—it reverses sign. On her own account she holds and adds to losers (averaging down); on the delegated account she sells losers and chases winners. A single loss-aversion parameter cannot produce this sign flip. It follows, we argue, from delegation changing what a return means: on her own account a loss is processed through realization utility, while on the delegated account it is both a signal about the algorithm and a source of dissonance she resolves by exiting. We estimate the model structurally and quantify the return investors forgo through self-directed loss aversion. Full empirical results remain in progress.

WORK IN PROGRESS

When Price Stops Clearing: Valuation Disagreement and Housing Illiquidity

Similar price-and-duration fits imply very different benefits from a 25% increase in valuation-signal precision: selling time falls 9.07% in the information-only model but barely changes in the combined model.

Abstract: Why do some homes sell slowly at high prices while others sell slowly at low prices? I combine Beijing housing transactions, repeated asking prices, and an estimated search-and-negotiation model. Both high and low recorded asks accompany long selling times, but the low-ask relationship reverses among homes with no recorded price adjustments. The price premium associated with few comparable sales largely disappears after conditioning on signed asks. Greater baseline follower interest predicts fewer subsequent price cuts. The model separates the return to meeting more buyers from the return to waiting for an optimistic offer. Joint estimation of prices, ask–sale gaps, and duration favors persistent differences in buyer access. With flexible quote errors, the combined fit places almost no weight on the information content of comparable counts. Yet an information-only specification has a close predictive score and implies a 9.07% reduction in selling time from a 25% increase in valuation-signal precision, compared with almost no change in the combined fit. Similar trading patterns can therefore support different valuations of information. The equilibrium extension identifies when lost transaction information makes fewer competing sellers slow agreement, and when an independent report can reverse that effect.

Presented at: Rising Scholar Conference in Finance (University of Zurich, 2026), UEA Summer School (LSE, 2026), USI IdEP Brown Bag (USI, 2024)

Grandfathering at Sale: Nonportable School Rights and Housing Exchange

After a sale resets school rights, relative sales growth is 32–45% lower and transaction prices 4–5% lower in formerly key-school neighborhoods; lower prices need not restore trade.

Abstract: Can lower prices sustain housing trade when buyers cannot acquire the school rights enjoyed by existing owners? I study a Beijing reform that preserved the previous admission rules for eligible homeowners, while new buyers faced assignment across several schools. Linking enrollment notices to housing transactions, I compare winter-to-spring changes around January 2019 with the same seasons in earlier years. Sales remain roughly flat in formerly key-school neighborhoods but rise in ordinary-school neighborhoods. After adjusting for earlier seasonal patterns, the gap in relative sales growth is 32–45 percent; transaction prices are also 4–5 percent lower relative to ordinary-school neighborhoods. A matching model explains why price reductions need not restore trade when a sale changes the rights attached to a home. The model also shows that portable school rights can facilitate exchange while reducing other families' access when school places are fixed.

WORK IN PROGRESS

When Jobs End: Layoffs, Quits, and Retirement Cash-Outs

Retirement cash-outs are elevated after both voluntary and involuntary job endings; adjusted gaps versus continuers are 1.95 and 2.78 percentage points, with an imprecise difference between separators.

Abstract: Employer-sponsored retirement accounts can make job endings labor-market and portfolio events. I study reported retirement lump sums for which no rollover was completed or planned in 39,481 SIPP person-years for workers with positive prior main-job earnings and prior 401(k) or thrift ownership. Rates are 0.78 percent among continuers, 2.61 among voluntary separators, and 3.68 among involuntary separators; adjusted separator–continuer gaps are 1.95 and 2.78 percentage points. The 0.83-point difference has a 95-percent interval [-0.47,2.13]. Non-rollover accounts for 68 and 77 percent of their additional receipt. Elevated non-rollover extends to voluntary mobility; its relation to displacement depends on the labor-market contrast.
